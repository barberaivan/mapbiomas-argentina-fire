\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Scope and purpose of this document}

This document describes the theoretical basis, the methodological framework and the operational
considerations behind Collection 1 of MapBiomas Argentina Fuego: an annual, 30 m burned-area
product covering the whole of Argentina for the period 1999--2025. It succeeds the ATBD of the
Patagonian pilot (Collection 0), whose structure and concepts it inherits, and whose algorithm
it replaces almost entirely.

\textbf{This ATBD is conceptual, and it is deliberately not a complete specification of the
algorithm.} Every stage described below carries dozens of decisions --- thresholds, window
lengths, encodings, tiling schemes, masks, edge cases --- and writing all of them down here would
produce a document several times this length that nobody would read. What follows is the
\emph{reasoning}: what each stage measures, why it measures it that way, and what it hands to the
next stage. Readers who want to reproduce the algorithm elsewhere, or who need the exact value of
a parameter, should go to the per-step technical notes in the project repository, at
\href{https://github.com/barberaivan/mapbiomas-argentina-fire/tree/main/collection-01/docs}{\texttt{collection-01/docs}}
(\url{https://github.com/barberaivan/mapbiomas-argentina-fire}). There is one note per workflow
step, numbered to match it, and each one points onward to the production code, the configuration
files and the analysis notebooks that justify its choices. Where this document says
\enquote{see \texttt{docs/NN}}, that is the file it means.

Validation and the statistical characterisation of the product are outside the scope of this
document. They are separate activities that \emph{consume} the finished map rather than being
stages of it, and they are documented on their own.

\subsection{Objectives of Collection 1}

Collection 0 had two goals: to assemble a working group capable of producing burned-area maps for
MapBiomas Argentina, and to develop a mapping algorithm of reasonable accuracy with a relatively
low demand of human effort for training-data collection. Both were met at the scale of one region.
Collection 1 sets out to

\begin{itemize}
  \item extend the mapping to the \textbf{entire national territory} and to a multi-decadal
        Landsat record;
  \item replace the parts of the pilot algorithm that did not generalise --- in particular the
        hand-drawn masks and the empirical object filter --- with \textbf{data-driven,
        reproducible components}; and
  \item deliver the set of burned-area subproducts that the MapBiomas Fuego network publishes for
        every participating country \parencite{alencar2022mapbiomas_fire}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Spatial and temporal extent}

Collection 1 maps the whole of Argentina, buffered by approximately 2 km so that no coastal or
border pixel is clipped, and works over the five regions defined by MapBiomas Argentina ---
Patagonia; Monte, Puna and Altos Andes (\enquote{Cuyo}); Chaco; Pampas; and Atlantic Forest
(Fig.~\ref{fig-regions}). The regions are not processing units in the sense that the pilot
anticipated: they enter the algorithm as a \emph{covariate} (Section~\ref{sec-vegfire}), while
the actual processing runs over the national 1:250,000 chart grid, the \emph{cartas}
(Section~\ref{sec-grid}), or --- from the object stage onwards --- over the whole country at once.

\FloatBarrier

The spectral stage evaluates Landsat observations from 1998 onwards and produces annual layers for
the calendar years \textbf{1999--2025}. The year 1998 is used only as the previous-year reference
that the annual metrics require. From the segmentation stage onwards the algorithm works in a
\textbf{fire year} running from 1 May to 30 April (Section~\ref{sec-fireyear}), which yields fire
years 1998--2025, the first and the last being partial by construction.

\subsection{Input datasets}

Collection 1 was developed on complete Landsat time series plus two ancillary MapBiomas Argentina
layers, processed almost entirely in Google Earth Engine
(GEE; \citeauthor{gorelick_google_2017}~\citeyear{gorelick_google_2017}).

\subsubsection{Landsat imagery}

All available Landsat Collection 2 Level-2 (surface reflectance) scenes at 30 m intersecting the
territory and the period were used \parencite{landsatc2}. During the mapped period the active
sensors were Landsat 5 TM (until 2013), Landsat 7 ETM+ (from mid-1999), Landsat 8 OLI (from 2013)
and Landsat 9 OLI-2 (from 2021). The six reflective bands --- blue, green, red, near infrared
(NIR) and the two shortwave infrared bands (SWIR1, SWIR2) --- are read, and a set of spectral
indices is derived from them (Section~\ref{sec-predictors}).

Scenes acquired on the same date are collapsed into a single date-mosaic before anything else
happens, so that the unit the algorithm reasons about is a \textbf{date}, not a scene footprint.
A \emph{carta} is covered by roughly 150 such dates in a padded focal year.

\subsubsection{Quality filters}

Landsat observations are masked with the QA\_PIXEL band delivered with the Collection 2 surface
reflectance products, excluding cloud, dilated cloud, cloud shadow, snow and water.

\textbf{No temporal interpolation and no spatial gap-filling are applied.} A missing observation
stays missing. This is a deliberate constraint rather than an omission: the temporal stage
(Section~\ref{sec-temporal}) reads real acquisition dates and counts real observations, and both
would be corrupted by synthetic values. The direct consequence is that observation density varies
in space and in time --- strongly so before 2000, when only Landsat 5 and a newly launched
Landsat 7 were flying --- and the algorithm carries that density forward as an explicit quality
channel rather than hiding it.

\subsubsection{Spectral harmonisation between sensors}

Because the series combines sensors with slightly different spectral responses, Landsat 5 TM and
Landsat 7 ETM+ reflectances are transformed into the Landsat 8/9 OLI domain band by band, using
the coefficients of \textcite{roy2016harmonization}. OLI and OLI-2 observations are left
untouched. This reduces artificial discontinuities at sensor transitions, which in a change
detector would otherwise be read as change.

\subsubsection{MapBiomas Argentina land cover}

The annual land use and land cover (LULC) classification of MapBiomas Argentina
\parencite{mapbiomasarg} is used for two distinct purposes, and both read the
\textbf{previous year} (\(y-1\)) relative to the year being mapped:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item it defines the \textbf{fire class} (\texttt{veg\_fire}) of every pixel, which selects
        which burn-probability model judges that pixel, and which surfaces are excluded from
        mapping altogether as non-burnable (Section~\ref{sec-vegfire});
  \item it supplies the object-level vegetation composition metrics used by the object classifier
        (Section~\ref{sec-objmetrics}).
\end{enumerate}

Reading \(y-1\) is not a detail. The land cover of a burned pixel changes \emph{because} it
burned, so using the focal year's classification would let the fire we are trying to detect decide
how it is detected. The same rule is applied everywhere the classification is consumed.

\subsubsection{MapBiomas annual mosaic}

The second ancillary layer is the MapBiomas Argentina \textbf{annual mosaic} --- the reflectance
summary that the land-cover initiative itself classifies. Forty of its bands are used: the six
optical bands plus NDVI, NDWI, NPV and NDFI \parencite{souza2005ndfi}, each summarised as annual
median, dry-season median, wet-season median and standard deviation. Again, the mosaic of the
\textbf{previous} year is attached to every observation.

This layer is what gives the burn-probability model its \emph{baseline}. A burn scar is not a
spectral value; it is a departure from what that particular pixel normally looks like, and how
large a departure counts depends on the pixel's productivity, seasonality and spectral
variability. The previous-year mosaic encodes all three, and it does so per pixel, which is what
allows a single model to work across an enormous productivity gradient. Collection 0 built the
same idea with hand-made robust annual minima and maxima of four indices; Collection 1 replaces
them with a layer that MapBiomas already computes, is already quality-controlled, and is far
richer.

\subsection{Computing platforms}

Most of the pipeline runs in GEE, which stores the entire Landsat archive and the MapBiomas
products and provides the compute to evaluate a model over them. Two stages run locally: the
fitting of the burn-probability models, in R \parencite{R} with \texttt{glmnet}
\parencite{friedman2010glmnet}, and the entire object stage --- vectorisation, object metrics and
the object classifier --- also in R, on tabular and raster data downloaded from GEE. Only
coefficients and object attributes cross between the two environments, never imagery.

The GEE compute project is shared with the whole MapBiomas Fuego network, which has one practical
consequence worth recording: every task launched by Argentina carries a namespaced description, and
no task is ever inspected or cancelled on the basis of a generic name.

\section{The burned-area mapping algorithm}

The algorithm detects burned area at the finest temporal and spatial resolution Landsat allows ---
one 30 m pixel on one observation date --- by exploiting the \textbf{spectral}, \textbf{temporal}
and \textbf{spatial} signature of fire, in that order. Each stage consumes what the previous one
produced and adds a kind of evidence the previous one could not see (Fig.~\ref{fig-algo}).

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{algorithm_c00_placeholder.png}
  \caption{\textbf{Placeholder.} The diagram of the Collection 0 algorithm, shown here while the
  Collection 1 diagram is being prepared. The overall shape --- spectral, then temporal, then
  spatial context, then the published subproducts --- still holds, but several boxes have changed:
  Collection 1 has no annual probabilistic classifier and no hand-drawn masks, and its object
  filter is a fitted classifier rather than a set of thresholds.}
  \label{fig-algo}
\end{figure}

\FloatBarrier

\begin{description}
  \item[Spectral --- is this observation burned?] A probabilistic classifier scores a single
    pixel on a single date, from its own reflectance and indices plus previous-year context. The
    output is a \textbf{burn probability per observation}. Nothing is decided here.
  \item[Temporal --- was this pixel burned this year, and when?] A burn probability on one date is
    not a detection: cloud shadow, wet soil and dark surfaces all raise it. What distinguishes
    fire is what the probability does \emph{through time} --- it rises and \emph{stays} risen. The
    second stage quantifies, per pixel and per calendar year, a \textbf{maintained increase} in
    burn probability, together with the date on which it happened.
  \item[Spatial --- is this pixel part of a fire?] Fires are patches, not independent pixels. The
    third stage brings that context in twice: a region-growing algorithm converts per-pixel
    evidence into scar \textbf{objects}, and a classifier then judges each object on properties no
    pixel has --- shape, size, vegetation composition, seed density, the spread of burn dates.
\end{description}

\subsection{The unifying principle: keep quantities, decide late}

One idea runs through all three stages: \textbf{quantitative information is preserved for as long
as possible}, so that every later step can weigh the earlier evidence instead of inheriting
someone else's verdict. The temporal stage analyses a burn probability, not a hard burned/unburned
class. The region-growing step judges pixels on numeric metrics, which is the only reason the
seed/candidate distinction can be expressed at all. The object classifier consumes continuous
metrics and returns a \emph{probability} of fire, which is used to filter but is also published,
so a user can apply a stricter or a looser threshold than ours. A pipeline that thresholded early
would be simpler, and would throw away exactly the evidence the next stage needs.

\subsection{The processing grid}\label{sec-grid}

Every image-based step runs over the MapBiomas Argentina working grid --- the national
1:250,000 chart series, locally called \emph{cartas}. Each sheet covers roughly
14,000 km\textsuperscript{2}, and 248 of them intersect the buffered country. Tiling by chart
sheet rather than by Landsat WRS-2 path/row is what lets one step read another step's tile and
assemble the results on a single lattice with no resampling anywhere in the chain. All exports
pin the projection and the affine transform explicitly, never a nominal scale, because in
geographic coordinates a nominal 30 m scale is a \emph{different} grid.

\section{Spectral stage: burn probability per observation}\label{sec-spectral}

\subsection{Foundations}

\textbf{The unit of judgement is the observation, not the year.} The model asks, for a single
pixel on a single date, how likely it is that this pixel is burned, using spectral information
only. Every usable Landsat observation is evaluated, so a fire is detected as a \emph{change in
the series} rather than as a signature in a summary image. The approach follows
\textcite{long2019}, where all Landsat observations are scored.

This is the opposite end of the design space from MapBiomas Fire Brazil, which classifies an
\textbf{annual mosaic} --- the same way land cover is mapped everywhere in the network.
Classifying a mosaic is cheaper and reuses the land-cover machinery, but it collapses the time
axis exactly where the information is: the date of the burn, the pre-fire baseline of that
specific pixel, and the distinction between a scar and a spectrally similar surface that was
always dark. Keeping the observation as the unit is what makes the temporal stage possible at all.

It is also a choice about \emph{time granularity}. There are methods that avoid discretising time
altogether: CCDC fits a continuous harmonic model per pixel and flags breaks in it
\parencite{zhu2014ccdc}, and CODED extends that idea to forest degradation with spectral unmixing
\parencite{bullock2020coded}. MapBiomas works in discrete annual units, and so does this
collection: the product is one burned-area map per year, so aggregating the raw Landsat
information by year is the natural shape for everything upstream of it.

The consequence for training data is direct: \textbf{a training point is not a label}. The label
is per observation, assigned from where that observation's date falls relative to the fire.

\subsection{Training data}

Training points were collected interactively by domain experts, fire by fire. For each selected
fire the collector delimits a pre-fire and a post-fire window on the Landsat record, and then
places burned and unburned points inside the scar and its surroundings, aiming to cover as much of
the spectral variability of each class as possible and inspecting index time series wherever the
classification is doubtful.

Collection 1 extends this protocol from 30 fires in one region to \textbf{198 fires across the
five regions} (13 in Atlantic Forest, 30 in Cuyo, 37 in Pampas, 47 in Patagonia, 71 in Chaco),
holding \textbf{147,222 points}. The full Landsat time series is then extracted at every point,
which turns those points into \textbf{6,177,098 observations}. Labels are assigned by window:
observations of a burned point after the fire are labelled burned; observations of a burned point
before the fire, and all observations of unburned points, are labelled unburned.

The efficiency argument made in the pilot ATBD holds and is worth restating, because it is the
reason this protocol was kept: each collected point yields roughly forty labelled observations, so
the sampling effort buys an order of magnitude more training data than placing one point per
observation would. Observations from the same point are temporally correlated, so the effective
gain is smaller than the nominal one --- but the time series is needed anyway, since it is the
series, not the single observation, that the rest of the algorithm reads.

\textbf{Fires with no burned points are collected on purpose.} Several of the sampled
\enquote{fires} are in fact drought-affected steppe or volcanic-ash deposits --- events that look
like fire in the indices and were the largest source of commission error in the pilot. They enter
the training set as unburned-only, and dropping them would remove the hardest negatives the model
ever sees.

\subsection{The cleaning gate}

Collectors make mistakes: a post-fire window set too generously, a date that belongs to the next
fire, an entire fire that should not be in the training set at all. A mandatory cleaning pass sits
between the download of the observations and the fit, adding a boolean \texttt{fit} column that
marks which observations the model is allowed to see; the fitting script refuses to run without
it.

The pass has two layers. A \textbf{base hard filter} keeps only observations inside the valid
window for the point's type, from the collector's own pre/post dates. On top of it sit
\textbf{per-fire manual edits}, transcribed from a reviewed workbook --- typically of the form
\enquote{keep only the first three post-fire dates} or \enquote{drop this fire}. The edits are
ranked over the fire's \emph{unique dates}, pooled across all of its burned points, not over each
point's own observation count; that distinction is the single most common way to mis-apply such a
rule.

Of the 6,177,098 extracted observations, \textbf{5,923,062 pass the gate}, of which
\textbf{541,789 (9.1\,\%) are labelled burned}. The edits are verified visually on per-fire panels
that plot the fitted burn probability against the raw indices and mark held-out dates
(Fig.~\ref{fig-ts-panel}).

\subsection{Fire classes: the \texttt{veg\_fire} remap}\label{sec-vegfire}

\textbf{A separate model is fitted per vegetation type}, because burn signatures differ by fuel:
what a scar does to the spectral series of a closed forest is not what it does in grassland or
open shrubland, and one pooled model would fit the average of surfaces that do not behave alike.

The unit of stratification is the \texttt{veg\_fire} class, obtained by crossing the MapBiomas
region with the MapBiomas LULC class of the previous year and remapping the result through a
single lookup table. The table yields \textbf{23 fittable classes} plus two non-fittable sentinels
--- \emph{non-burnable} (water, rock, ice, urban, bare) and \emph{non-observed}. Only the 23 are
ever predicted, so the non-burnable mask that the network's post-processing applies at the end is,
in this algorithm, applied at the very beginning.

The class names carry both the vegetation type and the region it belongs to:
\texttt{forest\_pat}, \texttt{grassland\_pampa}, \texttt{shrubland-open\_chaco},
\texttt{agriculture\_chaco}, and so on. \textbf{The grouping is not purely ecological --- data
availability was a hard constraint.} A strictly within-region remap was considered first, but
several classes are better off crossing regions: partly for spectral and land-use similarity,
partly because data collection was organised by region and that did not guarantee that every class
would be represented in every region. So a class may span regions ---
\texttt{agriculture\_cuyo-pat} covers both Cuyo and Patagonia. \textbf{The remap is an open path
for improvement}: with more collected fires, groups currently merged for want of data could be
split on their own merits.

Classes backed by few fires are fitted anyway rather than dropped or merged further; the
alternative removes a vegetation type from the map entirely. The cost is visible in the
cross-validation, where the number of folds is capped by the number of fires carrying positives.

\subsection{Model structure}

\textbf{The model has to be deployable inside GEE, and that constraint chose it.} A fitted
logistic regression is a set of coefficients plus a simple equation: trivial to store, to version
and to ship as a CSV, and cheap to evaluate over billions of pixel-dates. The richer classifiers
GEE offers natively --- random forest, boosted trees --- fail at both ends here. It is doubtful
they could be fitted on this many training observations, and a fitted one cannot be saved as an
asset, which is precisely what deploying over the whole Landsat archive requires. External
machine-learning services would lift both limits at a large step up in complexity and at a
monetary cost to the initiative.

We also wanted a \textbf{natively probabilistic} model, because everything downstream consumes a
probability rather than a hard class: the temporal metrics read the \emph{shape} of \(p\) through
time. The GEE constraint bites again --- a probability-mode random forest is not exportable, and
extracting calibrated probabilities from a regression-mode forest on binary data means tuning
minimum node size against \(N\), a dependency that would have to be re-tuned per class. Logistic
regression gives the probability by construction, with nothing to tune for it.

The probability that the observation of pixel \(c\) at time \(t\) corresponds to burned is

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-logistic}
  \begin{aligned}
    \Pr(y_{c,t} = 1) &= \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-\eta_{c,t})}, \\
    \eta_{c,t} &= \alpha_{k[c]} + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \beta_{j,k[c]}\, x_{j,c,t},
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are estimated parameters, \(k \in \{1,\dots,23\}\) is the
\texttt{veg\_fire} class, and the notation \(k[c]\) indicates the class of pixel \(c\) in the year
\emph{previous} to the one time \(t\) belongs to. \(J = 51\) is the number of regression terms in
the deployed model.

\textbf{Every class shares one predictor set}, chosen globally rather than per class: only the
coefficients differ between vegetation types. That keeps deployment cheap in the same way the
model itself does --- the prediction pipeline builds one band set once and reuses it for all 23
classes, instead of assembling a different design per vegetation type and paying for it at every
pixel-date.

\subsection{Predictors}\label{sec-predictors}

Twenty-one spectral features are computed for every Landsat observation: the six optical bands and
fifteen indices --- NBR \parencite{key_benson_2006}, NBR2
\parencite{usgs_landsat_burned_area_2021}, MIRBI \parencite{trigg_flasse_2001}, NDVI
\parencite{tucker_1979}, the three tasselled-cap components \parencite{baig2014tasseled}, NDMI
\parencite{gao1996ndwi}, NDSI \parencite{hall1995ndsi}, SAVI \parencite{huete1988savi}, NDWI
\parencite{mcfeeters1996ndwi}, AFRI \parencite{karnieli2001afri}, kNDVI
\parencite{campsvalls2021kndvi}, EVI2 \parencite{jiang2008evi2} and NIRv
\parencite{badgley2017nirv}. Forty previous-year mosaic bands are available alongside them.

Four fire-sensitive indices are worth writing out, since they are the ones the pilot used and the
ones that still carry most of the fire signal:

\begin{equation*}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{NBR}  &= \frac{\mathrm{NIR} - \mathrm{SWIR2}}{\mathrm{NIR} + \mathrm{SWIR2}}, &
    \mathrm{NBR2} &= \frac{\mathrm{SWIR1} - \mathrm{SWIR2}}{\mathrm{SWIR1} + \mathrm{SWIR2}}, \\[4pt]
    \mathrm{NDVI} &= \frac{\mathrm{NIR} - \mathrm{RED}}{\mathrm{NIR} + \mathrm{RED}}, &
    \mathrm{NDMI} &= \frac{\mathrm{NIR} - \mathrm{SWIR1}}{\mathrm{NIR} + \mathrm{SWIR1}}.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

The candidate design was much larger than the deployed one. It began as several hundred terms ---
all focal features, all mosaic summaries and a large set of interactions --- and was reduced in two
steps, first to 129 terms and then, by ranking those terms globally and cutting the ranking, to the
deployed set. Predictive skill is flat from the full fit down to about fifty terms and falls below
that, so the smallest set on the flat part was deployed. The reduction is not cosmetic: \emph{the
number of terms is the dominant cost of the prediction stage}, since each term becomes one image
band that must be built and multiplied at every pixel-date. The deployed set costs about 25\,\%
less compute per tile than the 129-term one.

The deployed model carries an intercept plus 51 terms, in five blocks:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{10 focal main effects} --- GREEN, RED, NIR, SWIR1, SWIR2, NBR, NBR2, NDVI, NDMI,
        NDSI, all measured on the observation being scored;
  \item \textbf{14 previous-year mosaic main effects} --- medians, dry/wet composites and standard
        deviations of the optical bands and of NDVI, NDWI, NPV and NDFI;
  \item \textbf{10 focal\,\(\times\)\,focal interactions} --- e.g.\ NBR\,\(\times\)\,NBR2,
        SWIR1\,\(\times\)\,NDVI;
  \item \textbf{4 same-band interactions} --- a focal band with its own previous-year median
        (GREEN, NIR, SWIR2, NDVI), which is the model's explicit \emph{change} term: the product of
        today's value with this pixel's baseline;
  \item \textbf{13 cross interactions} between previous-year mosaic summaries and focal fire
        indices or focal bands.
\end{itemize}

The design deserves a word, because it is what makes a linear model work across a country. A
logistic regression on raw indices alone would be exactly the \enquote{one threshold everywhere}
approach that the pilot ATBD argued against; the optimal NBR cut for a Patagonian forest is not the
optimal cut for Chaco open shrubland. The previous-year mosaic main effects let the model shift
its intercept with the pixel's normal appearance, and the cross and same-band interactions let it
change the \emph{slope} of its response to a fire index according to that appearance. Fitting a
separate model per \texttt{veg\_fire} class handles the between-type differences; the interactions
with the previous-year baseline handle the within-type gradient in productivity, structure and
seasonality.

\subsection{Fitting, regularisation and cross-validation}

The design is strongly collinear by construction --- indices share bands, interactions share
factors --- so the models are fitted by \textbf{elastic net} \parencite{zou2005elasticnet} with
\texttt{glmnet} \parencite{friedman2010glmnet}, maximising the penalised Bernoulli likelihood. The
mixing parameter \(\alpha\) is tuned over \(\{0.25, 0.5, 0.75\}\) --- pure ridge and pure lasso were
dropped after never winning --- and \(\lambda\) is selected at the cross-validated minimum.

\textbf{Cross-validation holds out whole fires.} Observations from a single fire are strongly
correlated in space and in time; splitting them across folds would report a skill the model does
not have on a new fire. Folds are therefore grouped on a region-unique fire identifier, with
stratified packing so that folds carry comparable numbers of positives, and \(K\) is adaptive:
\(K = \min(10, \text{fires with positives})\). Twenty-one of the 23 classes reach \(K = 10\); two
fit at 7 and 6. Out-of-fold predictions are stored per observation and are what the per-class
diagnostics --- calibration, omission and commission, by-fire breakdowns --- are computed on.

Interactions are fitted on mean-centred factors for numerical stability and then folded back to
the raw-product scale when the coefficients are exported, so that the deployed model is a plain
dot product with no centring step.

\subsection{Deployment in Earth Engine}\label{sec-deploy}

The fitted models reach GEE as one small CSV of coefficients per class. Turning 23 such tables into
a prediction over a whole country raises one design question, and the answer shapes the rest of the
pipeline.

The obvious implementation --- evaluate each class's model on its own masked subset and mosaic the
results --- builds 23 parallel branches of the same computation graph. Instead, \textbf{the class
selection is pushed into the data}: every term becomes one image band whose per-pixel value is
\emph{that pixel's class's coefficient} for that term, written by remapping the \texttt{veg\_fire}
image through the 23 coefficient values. A pixel of class 7 then carries class 7's coefficient for
every term, and its neighbour of class 12 carries class 12's, in the same band. Prediction becomes
a single arithmetic expression, identical everywhere, and choosing the model costs nothing at
evaluation time. This is also why the predictor set has to be shared across classes in the first
place.

Two further properties of the deployment are worth recording. First, every multiplication renames
its operands to identical band names before multiplying, rather than relying on band-matching
rules, so a coefficient can never be silently paired with the wrong band. Second, a masked pixel is
\textbf{masked, never zero}: cloud, no-data and non-burnable pixels contribute nothing at all, they
are not observations with probability zero.

\textbf{The per-observation probability is never written to an asset.} Over a padded year a carta
is covered by roughly 150 dates, so storing the probability per observation would mean about 150
layers per tile-year where the annual summary that follows has 16 bands --- an order of magnitude
more data than a product that is already on the order of a terabyte. And nothing downstream wants
a single observation's probability: what carries the evidence is the \emph{shape} of the series.
Since the model is a coefficient set plus a dot product, recomputing it inside the graph is far
cheaper than writing it out and reading it back. The spectral and temporal stages are therefore two
concepts but one piece of code.

\section{Temporal stage: burn-probability time-series metrics}\label{sec-temporal}

\subsection{Foundations}

For every focal year and every carta, this stage reads the burn probability of every Landsat
observation of every pixel and reduces that per-pixel series to a \textbf{16-band annual summary}:
how high the probability got, how persistently it stayed there, how sharply it jumped, when, and on
how many observations the answer rests. It never decides that a pixel burned. It measures the
evidence that the spatial stage segments and the object model classifies.

\textbf{This is the fire team's annual mosaic --- except that it is a prediction, not an
aggregation.} Every MapBiomas initiative reduces the raw Landsat archive to one annual layer per
tile and builds everything downstream on it; for land cover that layer is the annual mosaic, a
summary of \emph{reflectance}. This layer is the fire equivalent, one level up, with a difference
that decides a great deal: the model has already been applied, so what gets annualised is a
\emph{burn probability}. That buys more per byte --- the vegetation-specific model, the
previous-year context and the cloud masking are all resolved once, and no later stage ever touches
a Landsat scene again --- and it costs a dependency the plain mosaic does not have: this layer
inherits the model, its training data and the land-cover collection that defines
\texttt{veg\_fire}, so improving any of the three means recomputing it.

\textbf{Burn probability has to be smoothed before it is read as detection.} A single high
observation is as likely to be shadow, ash or a wet scene as fire. Collection 0 smoothed with a
median over five observations. Here, vegetation types recover at very different speeds and image
density varies by pixel and by year, so the stage carries \textbf{two shorter windows} --- \(K=3\)
and \(K=2\) --- and exports both, leaving the choice between them to the next stage, per pixel.

\textbf{The evidence is three things: magnitude, persistence and change}, all measured in
\emph{numbers of valid observations} and never in elapsed time, because the Landsat series is
irregular and its density is itself informative.

\subsection{The jump, and why it is defined that way}

Let \(\{p_t\}\) be the series of burn probabilities of a pixel, ordered by date, and let \(d_t\) be
the day number of observation \(t\) counted from 1 January of the focal year --- so that padding
observations from the previous year are negative, those from the next year exceed 365, and every
difference is an exact whole-day count across the year boundary.

For a window of \(K\) observations, define

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-delta}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{minfore}_K[t] &= \min\left(p_t, \dots, p_{t+K-1}\right), \\
    \mathrm{maxback}_K[t] &= \max\left(p_{t-K}, \dots, p_{t-1}\right), \\
    \mathrm{delta}_K[t]   &= \mathrm{minfore}_K[t] - \mathrm{maxback}_K[t].
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The asymmetry between the two operators is the core of the method. Taking the \textbf{minimum}
forward demands that the high signal be \emph{sustained}: a single isolated spike does not
qualify, because one low value among the next \(K\) observations collapses the floor. Taking the
\textbf{maximum} backward demands that the pixel was \emph{cleanly} low before: one high previous
value is enough to cancel the jump, which is what stops a noisy low observation inside an
already-burned scar from manufacturing a second detection of the same fire. \(\mathrm{delta}_K\)
is therefore the most conservative reading of a step that the series admits.

Alongside the jump, the stage records the time structure around it (Fig.~\ref{fig-bpts3}):
\(\mathrm{jumpgap}\), the number of days between the last low observation and the first high one;
\(\mathrm{prevwidth}\) and \(\mathrm{postwidth}\), the spans in days of the back and fore windows;
and \(\mathrm{date\_post}\), the day of year of the post-jump observation. \(\mathrm{jumpgap}\) is
strong fire evidence when \emph{short}: a long one may be slow change --- drought, dieback,
harvest --- rather than fire, or a fire seen through a thin image series. The two widths report how
much calendar time the windows actually cover, which under sparse imagery can be most of a season.

\subsection{The peak, and what is stored}

Each \(\mathrm{delta}_K\) is collapsed \textbf{independently} to the observation \(t^{*}\) at which
it is maximal within the focal year, and its whole bundle of companion values is read at that
observation. The two families are collapsed separately on purpose: the optimal transition
observation differs by window, and tying both to one peak would mis-anchor the other.

Three further bands are not tied to any peak --- the maximum raw probability of the year, and the
maxima of \(\mathrm{minfore}_2\) and \(\mathrm{minfore}_3\) over the whole series, which measure
sustained magnitude regardless of whether it arrived as a jump. Finally, \(n\), the count of valid
focal observations, is exported as the \textbf{sole density and quality channel}; it also carries
sentinel values marking non-burnable and non-observed pixels, and it is the one band that is never
masked.

Table~\ref{tab-bpts-bands} lists the 16 bands. All are encoded as signed 16-bit integers ---
probabilities scaled by \(10^4\), days as days --- which roughly halves the size of the asset.
Missing data is never given a probability: a pixel with no valid observations comes back with
\(n = 0\) and everything else masked. A pixel whose series is too short for a given window has
\emph{that window's} bands masked, which is a quality flag rather than an error.

Note that \(\mathrm{maxback}_K\) is not stored: it is exactly
\(\mathrm{minfore}_K - \mathrm{delta}_K\) and is recovered whenever it is needed. Note also that
\texttt{date\_post} is \emph{not} the fire date. It is the day of the post-jump observation, and
the burn date used downstream is the mid-point between the last low and the first high
observation, \(\mathrm{date\_post}_2 - \mathrm{jumpgap}_2/2\).

\subsection{Crossing the year boundary}

Window metrics starve at the extremes of a series --- \(\mathrm{maxback}_3\) needs three
observations behind it, \(\mathrm{minfore}_3\) two ahead --- and in a fire-prone country the start
and the end of the calendar year are in the middle of the burning season. The focal year therefore
\textbf{borrows three observations from the previous year and two from the next}: asymmetric on
purpose, because symmetric padding of two would under-supply the back window at the first focal
observation.

The neighbouring observations cannot simply be taken from the adjacent scenes, since the literal
nearest scene may be cloud-masked at this pixel. Burn probability is instead computed for a margin
of a few months on each side and the three latest previous and two earliest next \emph{valid}
observations are kept per pixel; the margin is widened for 1999 and 2000, where the archive is
thin. Padded observations use the focal year's previous-year context, which is strictly the wrong
year for them but is the better approximation over three-plus-two observations.

Crucially, \textbf{the search for \(t^{*}\) is always restricted to focal observations}. Padding is
only ever \emph{context}, so a burn can never be detected twice in two adjacent years --- which
means that, unlike the pilot, this collection has no cross-year duplicates to remove later.

\subsection{Cost}

This is the most expensive artifact in the collection, in compute and in storage: a complete year
is roughly 55 GiB over the 248 cartas, and the 27 years are on the order of 1.4 TiB. Per-account
task parallelism in GEE is limited, so a whole-country year cannot be produced by one person in
reasonable time; production ran as a distributed, multi-account export, one contributor per year,
with an idempotent launcher that skips any tile which already has a finished asset or a running
task.

Two consequences follow for anyone planning a change. First, the optimisation levers are all
\emph{product-level} --- number of terms, window length, band set --- and each one changes the
model or the output; there are no late tweaks to be had, because profiling puts the entire array
and time-series machinery below 1\,\% of per-tile cost, with the expense sitting in the model
arithmetic and the cloud masking over roughly 150 scenes. Second, \textbf{re-running this layer is
a collection-level decision}, not a step-level one.

\section{Spatial stage I: segmentation into scar objects}\label{sec-snic}

\subsection{Foundations}

\textbf{Seeds and candidates are how this stage avoids deciding early.} A single probability cut
would force one threshold to be both sensitive and specific. Instead a \emph{strict} cut defines
where a scar certainly started --- the \textbf{seeds} --- and a \emph{permissive} cut defines where
one could plausibly extend --- the \textbf{candidates} --- and region growing resolves the two: a
candidate joins a scar only if it is connected to a seed, and a candidate island with no seed in
it is dropped altogether (Fig.~\ref{fig-region-growing}).

This exploits a favourable trade-off between the two error types. Seeds have high omission and low
commission error; candidates the opposite. A pixel whose own evidence is weak is accepted when it
belongs to a patch whose evidence is strong, and rejected when it stands alone. Region growing has
been used this way in burned-area mapping for decades, exactly because fire is spatially contagious
\parencite{adams_bischof_1994,haralick_shapiro_1985,fraser2000,giglio2009,hawbaker2020,long2019}.

The stage errs deliberately towards \textbf{recall}: precision is recovered at the object
classifier of Section~\ref{sec-objmodel}, which can see the patch's shape, size and seed density
--- things no pixel can report.

\subsection{The fire year}\label{sec-fireyear}

\textbf{A fire is an object in space \emph{and} in time, and this stage buys the time axis with a
calendar rather than with an algorithm.} Segmenting calendar years independently splits every scar
that straddles 31 December and duplicates it across two years. The obvious repairs --- a temporal
firebreak inside the segmentation, a backward gap-fill from the previous year --- were built first
and did not work, because the per-pixel dates are too noisy to serve as a barrier. The problem
disappears entirely if the year boundary is placed where almost nothing is burning.

The \textbf{fire year} therefore runs from \textbf{1 May of year \(Y_1\) to 30 April of year
\(Y_1+1\)} and is named after its starting year. May is the country-wide trough of fire activity
--- in MODIS and VIIRS active fires and in our own burn dates --- so no region's season is cut in
half: the summer burners of the south and centre and the winter--spring season of the north both
fall inside a single May-to-April year. One national boundary is a compromise; Patagonia would
ideally break in June or July. Defining a separate fire year per region was tried and abandoned,
because processing the whole country in one pass brought far more benefit than the optimum boundary
would have.

Since the annual metrics cover calendar years 1999--2025, the fire years mapped are 1998--2025,
with two \textbf{partial} edges: fire year 1998 holds only the January--April 1999 tail, and fire
year 2025 only the May--December 2025 head. Both are flagged as partial in the asset metadata, and
anything that averages across fire years has to know.

\subsection{Seeds, candidates and dates}

A fire year spans two calendar-year metric images, and each is thresholded before they are
combined.

\textbf{Candidates} are pixels whose \(\mathrm{delta}_2\) exceeds a permissive cut --- always the
\(K=2\) family, because it gives the broadest footprint. \textbf{Seeds} are pixels whose
\(\mathrm{delta}_K\) exceeds a strict cut, with \textbf{\(K\) chosen per pixel} from its
vegetation class and its observation count \(n\): a pixel whose series is dense enough for its
class is judged on the conservative \(K=3\) metric, and a pixel in a thin part of the archive on
the permissive \(K=2\) one. A temporal-gap gate also applies, rejecting seeds whose
\(\mathrm{jumpgap}\) exceeds an \(n\)-adaptive ceiling --- that is, rejecting changes too slow to
be fire, which is precisely the drought signature that forced hand-drawn masks in the pilot. Cuts
are set per vegetation class, with global defaults, and non-vegetated classes receive an
unreachable cut so that they can never fire.

Each pixel's \textbf{burn date} is taken from the \(K=2\) family as
\(\mathrm{date\_post}_2 - \mathrm{jumpgap}_2/2\), converted to an absolute day count since a fixed
epoch, so that every later date comparison is safe across year boundaries.

The two calendar images are then \textbf{filtered by date and combined}. Only pixels whose burn
date falls inside \([\,1\ \mathrm{May}\ Y_1,\ 1\ \mathrm{May}\ Y_1{+}1)\) are kept: the \(Y_1\)
image contributes May--December, the \(Y_1{+}1\) image January--April, and a detection dated before
May of \(Y_1\) belongs to the previous fire year and is dropped. Where both images claim a pixel,
they are combined by rank --- seed beats candidate beats nothing --- and the date follows the image
that won. This is what turns two calendar layers into one non-calendar fire year.

\subsection{Slow post-fire dieback in Andean Patagonia}

Andean Patagonian forest dies \emph{slowly} after fire: part of a real scar crosses the change
thresholds only in the \emph{following} fire year, when the canopy finally collapses. For forest
and shrubland pixels west of a longitude cut, a pixel that is seed or candidate in the next
calendar image with a mid-winter to spring date is added to the focal year as a \textbf{candidate},
flagged with its own code --- never as a seed, because dieback must be able to \emph{extend} a
detected scar but never to \emph{create} one. Since padding survives region growing only where it
connects to a genuine focal seed, it cannot manufacture a fire. These pixels carry a next-year
date, so they are excluded from every date statistic downstream and inherit their parent object's
date instead.

\subsection{Supervised region growing}

The region growing itself uses SNIC (Simple Non-Iterative Clustering,
\citeauthor{AchantaSNIC}~\citeyear{AchantaSNIC}), as implemented in GEE. SNIC is an unsupervised
segmentation method, and its most common use is to partition a wall-to-wall image from uniformly
placed seeds and spectral features. Here it is used in a far more guided way: we supply the seeds
and we supply the footprint through which they may grow, so what would ordinarily be a
segmentation becomes a classification --- SNIC is the mechanism, and the seed/candidate definition
is the model.

Two details matter. Seed clumps below a small size are \textbf{demoted to candidates} before
growing: they no longer seed, but they remain in the footprint so that a genuine cluster can still
grow through them. And the growing runs on the \textbf{whole country in one pass}, not
region by region: SNIC's memory footprint depends on its internal tile size and neighbourhood
buffer, not on the extent of the export, so a national run costs what a regional one costs, and a
large neighbourhood heals the internal seams. Only the \emph{mask} of the result is kept --- the
cluster identifiers are discarded, because the objects are relabelled globally in the next stage.

The output is one image per fire year with a single band distinguishing candidate, seed and dieback
padding, masked everywhere else. Burned pixels are 0.3--1\,\% of the country, which is what makes
the whole national layer small enough to download.

\section{Spatial stage II: fire objects and the object classifier}\label{sec-objects}

\subsection{From pixels to objects}

\textbf{This stage materialises the fire patch so that it can be judged as a patch.} A patch has
properties no pixel has --- shape, size, vegetation composition, a spread of burn dates --- and
those are what separate a real scar from a field of spectrally similar noise.

Objects are the connected components of the burned pixels, labelled \textbf{one fire year at a
time, whole-country and untiled}, so that an object is global within its year and a scar is never
split by a processing seam. Labelling uses a union-find structure \parencite{tarjan1975uf}, which
needs only one integer per burned cell; the country's 30 m lattice holds 9.16 billion cells while a
heavy fire year holds around 116 million burned ones, three orders of magnitude fewer, and every
stage of this part of the pipeline is sized by the latter.

\textbf{Connectivity is wider than eight-neighbour adjacency, and selectively so.} A real scar
frequently breaks into fragments one or two pixels apart, and gluing them recovers one fire instead
of five. The rule is equivalent to dilating the burned mask by one pixel, labelling, and dropping
the halo --- but it is implemented as a wider union window, which avoids ever materialising the
dilation. Two burned cells are joined if their Chebyshev distance is at most 3 when both have
\emph{enlarged context}, at most 2 when only one does, and at most 1 --- plain eight-connectivity
--- when neither does. A cell is denied enlarged context when it is agriculture, pasture or
temperate grassland, where bridging distinct burned fields would inflate commission error, or when
it is a dieback pixel, which may only extend a scar. Everything else --- forests, shrublands, the
arid grasslands --- keeps it, because in sparse fuels bridging recovers a single real scar.

Each labelled object is then vectorised into one, possibly multi-part, polygon. Disconnected
fragments joined by the bridging rule dissolve into a single multipolygon, so the bridge is
preserved natively in the geometry.

\subsection{Object metrics}\label{sec-objmetrics}

The metric set is deliberately narrow --- only what the classifier uses --- and is computed
directly over the burned cells rather than over the polygons, which is both faster and exact for
area.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Seed fraction}: the share of the object's pixels that are seeds. This is the single
        most discriminating metric for fire against noise: real scars are densely seeded, noise is
        not.
  \item \textbf{Neighbourhood density}: the mean, over the object's pixels, of the burned fraction
        within square windows of one, two and three pixels' radius. Sparse, threadlike objects
        score low; solid scars score high.
  \item \textbf{Size}: area in hectares, and pixel count.
  \item \textbf{Dates}: the median, minimum and maximum burn date of the object's pixels, and the
        calendar year that most of its pixels fall in.
  \item \textbf{Vegetation composition}: the fraction of the object's pixels in each
        \texttt{veg\_fire} class.
  \item \textbf{Shape}: perimeter, convexity (area over convex-hull area), bounding-box fill and
        elongation, circularity \(4\pi A/P^2\) and shape index \(P/(2\sqrt{\pi A})\).
\end{itemize}

Area is measured on the ellipsoid, which means a pixel is not 0.09 ha: at these latitudes one
Landsat pixel covers about 831 m\textsuperscript{2} at 22°S and 517 m\textsuperscript{2} at 55°S.
A size class is therefore a \emph{range} of pixel counts, and the same 15-pixel object changes size
class between the north of the country and Patagonia.

\subsection{Object labels}\label{sec-objlabels}

The classifier is fitted on \textbf{about five thousand fire / non-fire labels collected by hand}.
Collectors work in the GEE Code Editor on the seed-and-candidate layer itself --- not on uploaded
polygons --- because that layer already shows, per pixel, candidate against seed on exactly the
clusters the region growing kept, which makes shape and seed density, the most informative signal,
fully visible without any vector data. Each collaborator exports a single asset; drawing-layer
names carry the class and the fire year. Labels are then matched to \emph{objects}, not to pixels,
and the object inherits the label.

Cleaning the label--object pairs --- dropping labels that hit no object, objects labelled both ways,
and duplicate labels on an already-labelled object --- yields a fitting set of \textbf{5,255
objects}, 2,788 fire and 2,467 non-fire.

\textbf{The labelled sample is not a random sample of objects}, and this single fact conditions
every number the model produces. Labels were collected where fires were known to be, so per-year
prevalence in the fitting set runs from 0.00 to 1.00, seven fire years have no labels at all, and
small objects are strongly under-sampled. The model's \emph{ranking} and its \emph{uncertainty} are
the product, not a calibrated probability; the deployed decision thresholds are a lower bound; and
the cross-validation design has to be chosen with this sampling in mind. Fixing it is a
\emph{collection} task, not a modelling one: a randomly sampled set of small-object labels.

\subsection{The classifier}\label{sec-objmodel}

\textbf{A classifier, not a threshold filter.} Collection 0 decided fire with a hand-built rule
over size, shape and neighbourhood density. Reproduced verbatim against our labels, that filter
scores an accuracy of 0.62 and a sensitivity of 0.50, against 0.81 and 0.85 for the model at the
deployed cuts --- and it fails worst exactly where the objects are, discarding 81\,\% of the real
fires in the 1--50 ha band, which holds 83\,\% of all objects. Two of its ideas survive: a hard
minimum-size cut, and reasoning stratified by size.

\textbf{The model is a probit BART} \parencite{chipman2010bart,albert1993probit}, fitted locally in
R with \texttt{stochtree} \parencite{stochtree}. The choice follows from what five thousand labels
allow. There is no honest way to tune a boosted ensemble's hyperparameters on a set this small,
whereas BART's defaults are calibrated regularisation priors rather than placeholders. More
importantly, the posterior yields a \textbf{per-object interval} on the probability --- both an
uncertainty statement that can be published and the targeting signal for a second round of label
collection. The fit takes about ninety seconds; scoring the whole object set is done fire year by
fire year in blocks, so that peak memory is one block however many objects exist.

The model is applied to \textbf{all 28 fire years and 1,689,419 objects}.

\subsection{Predictors, and why none may name the year}

Twenty predictors are used: fifteen size, shape, density, seed and date-structure metrics, plus
\textbf{five aggregated vegetation fractions} --- agriculture, flooded grassland, pasture,
temperate grassland and woody. The aggregation replaced the 23 raw class fractions, which were
58\,\% of the design matrix and consumed the model's split budget without adding skill; membership
of each group is derived from the remap table \emph{by name}, so a remap change follows through
automatically. The five groups are deliberately not a partition and do not sum to one.

\textbf{No predictor may name the year or act as a proxy for it}, and the rule has already cost
three candidates. Per-year label prevalence in the fitting set is an artifact of where people drew,
so a model given the year learns that sampling pattern and applies it to every object of that year.
The fire year and the calendar year were removed for leaking the labels outright --- out of fold,
the prediction for a year \emph{was} its label prevalence --- and the mean observation count was
removed as a \emph{soft era proxy}: observation density rises as sensors come online, which lets an
improving archive masquerade as a rising fire regime. All three keep their roles as product
attributes; none of them is a predictor.

The same rule is why season enters as \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) of the day of year rather than as the
day itself --- season is legitimate information, but it must be \textbf{circular}, since the fire
season straddles the turn of the year --- and why no absolute time coordinate is a predictor, burn
dates included. Note that spatially blocked cross-validation cannot detect this class of leak,
because it blocks space and leaves the year on both sides of every split; a standing per-year check
is run instead.

\subsection{The decision threshold}

\textbf{A cut of 0.5 is wrong, and the right cut rises with object size.} Thresholds are swept on
\emph{out-of-fold} probabilities --- never in-sample, or the cut would be chosen against answers the
model has already seen --- and selected by Youden's \(J\) \parencite{youden1950}, which is the only
criterion here that does not move with prevalence.

The bootstrap intervals on the three deployed cuts are close to disjoint, so the differences are
signal rather than noise: the model is far more confident on large objects, and a single threshold
would be simultaneously too high for small objects and too low for large ones. The gain lands where
the error was --- sensitivity in the 1--50 ha band rises from 0.596 to 0.837.

Two qualifications. First, \textbf{the threshold governs object counts, not the area headline}: on
a heavy year the band cuts call some 64,000 objects fire against 50,500 at a flat 0.5, for an
increase of well under 2\,\% in burned area, because area is dominated by large objects the model
is confident about. Second, \textbf{the cuts are a lower bound}. \(J\) is prevalence-invariant as a
\emph{measure}, but the cut it selects is optimal for the prevalence of the set it was selected on,
and that set is not the population (Section~\ref{sec-objlabels}).

Where a human label exists for an object, it \textbf{overrides} the model's call. The published
call is therefore the collected label where there is one and the model's call otherwise, and the
three columns are kept separate in the product so that the provenance of every call is visible.

\subsection{Performance}

Cross-validation blocks space into 0.5° cells assigned to five folds. This removes the adjacency
leak --- objects from one drawn polygon are neighbours --- while keeping every region in every fold,
which is the deployment condition. Leave-one-region-out was run for comparison and is the harshest
possible test, but it is not the condition the model is used in: every region does have labels in
production, and held-out prevalence swings from 0.10 in Patagonia to 0.83 in the Pampas, so a
fold's model would be trained on a class mix it is never scored on. \textbf{The fold design decides
the answer}, and the choice has to be argued rather than defaulted.

Performance is not uniform across sizes. Above 300 ha the model is nearly clean (AUC 0.923); the
weak band is \textbf{1--50 ha} (AUC 0.871), which is also where 83\,\% of the objects are, and
where at a flat 0.5 cut the model would miss two fires in five. That band is 61\,\% of the labels
but 83\,\% of the population --- the same under-sampling that the threshold caveat and the posterior
widths both report.

Variable importance was assessed with four different measures, because the predictors are strongly
correlated --- area with pixel count with perimeter, the three nested neighbourhood windows --- and
each measure mishandles correlation in its own way. Partial-dependence curves were avoided for the
same reason and \textbf{accumulated local effects} \parencite{apley2020ale} used instead: a partial
dependence plot averages over combinations that do not exist, such as a one-pixel object with a ten
kilometre perimeter, and invents effects there. \textbf{Two predictors carry the model}: the
temperate-grassland fraction and the seed fraction. That is the intended story --- real scars are
densely seeded, and the fuel type decides how a burned patch looks. The size and shape block is
nearly inert, and pixel count is last of the twenty on every measure. Reassuringly, no predictor
shows the signature that exposed the year leak: a large split share concentrated near the root of
the trees with an effect that tracks the calendar.

\subsection{The minimum mapped size}

Scoring the full population answers the question the minimum-size decision was supposed to rest on
--- \emph{is the model measurably less able to classify small objects?} --- and the answer is: not
distinctively. Posterior uncertainty does fall monotonically with size, with mean interval width
going from 0.41 below half a hectare to 0.13 above a thousand hectares, but the model is
\textbf{unsure everywhere}: the global mean width is 0.32 and 31\,\% of all objects have an
interval that straddles their own decision cut.

Two conclusions follow. \textbf{The honest argument for a one-hectare minimum is cost and benefit,
not uncertainty}: it discards 3.4\,\% of the objects for 0.044\,\% of the area. And the interval
width is reporting \emph{covariate shift} --- BART widens its posterior where it has no data, and
31\,\% undecided is that message about five thousand labels against 1.69 million objects. The
one-hectare cut is applied as a hard rule, which also satisfies, more strictly, the solitary-pixel
filter of the network's common post-processing, since one hectare is about eleven pixels.

\section{Products}\label{sec-products}

Steps one to six are the algorithm; what follows is delivery. The fire-year objects are
re-partitioned into \textbf{calendar years}, with the year and the month assigned \textbf{per pixel}
from that pixel's own burn date rather than per object, and the published layers are derived from
those pixels. Two of the network's post-processing stages --- the non-burnable land-cover mask and
the solitary-pixel filter --- are already embedded further upstream in this algorithm and more
strictly than the specification asks, so running them again would be a no-op at best.

\subsection{The six subproducts}

\textbf{The published products are the ones the MapBiomas Fuego network defines.} They are not
Argentina's design and they are not justified here: every participating country publishes the same
set, in the same shape and with the same pixel encodings, so that the layers are comparable across
the network and readable by the same platform. Argentina delivers all six --- \emph{annual},
\emph{monthly}, \emph{accumulated}, \emph{frequency}, \emph{year of last fire} and \emph{scar size};
some countries deliver only the first four.

The six become twelve assets: four have a \emph{coverage} variant crossing the burned pixels with
the published MapBiomas Argentina land cover of the same year, and the scar-size product comes with
the scar identifier and the scar area it derives from. Every asset is a \textbf{single multiband
image with one band per year}, not one image per year --- 27 bands (1999--2025) for the annual,
monthly and year-of-last-fire products, 53 for the frequency and accumulated ones, one per two-sided
window (Table~\ref{tab-products}).

A \textbf{scar} is a connected group of burned pixels within one calendar year, labelled with plain
eight-connectivity --- the network's definition, and deliberately not the wider connectivity the fire
objects of Section~\ref{sec-objects} use. The eight size classes are the platform's published legend:
\(<\)10, 10--250, 250--500, 500--5,000, 5,000--10,000, 10,000--50,000, 50,000--100,000 and
\(\ge\)100,000 ha. Argentina populates all eight; the largest scar in the series is 219,410 ha, in
2003. The scars also exist as vectors, one FeatureCollection per calendar year.

Two reading rules follow from how the pixels are built. \textbf{The month of burn is measured, not
inferred}: it comes from the pixel's own burn date rather than from the date of a minimum-NBR
composite, which is how most of the network derives it. And \textbf{a fire that straddles 31 December
is two scars}, one in each calendar year --- the price of assigning the date per pixel, and what
makes the annual, monthly and scar-size layers agree pixel for pixel.

\subsection{The fire-object vector database}\label{sec-polygons}

Alongside the six, and not one of them, Argentina publishes the \textbf{fire objects themselves} as a
single vector layer: \texttt{burned\_area\_polygons\_v2}, \textbf{1,012,645 objects covering
63.33 Mha}, every mapped fire of all 28 fire years in one FeatureCollection. It is the output of
Section~\ref{sec-objects} under the published selection, with nothing recomputed and no geometry
touched.

Each polygon carries ten properties: \texttt{oid}, the stable object identifier that joins back to
the object database and its twenty metrics; \texttt{fire\_year}, the non-calendar mapping year;
\texttt{calendar\_year}; \texttt{area\_ha}; the three burn dates \texttt{date\_med},
\texttt{date\_min} and \texttt{date\_max}, written as \texttt{YYYY-MM-DD}; \texttt{p\_mean} and
\texttt{p\_width}, the object classifier's posterior mean fire probability and the width of its
credible interval; and \texttt{seed\_mean}. Every feature is stamped with
\texttt{system:time\_start} from \texttt{date\_med}, so the collection answers \texttt{filterDate()};
\texttt{system:time\_end} is deliberately not set, so that one fire falls in exactly one time bucket
and filtered areas stay summable.

\textbf{Three things a user of this layer must know.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{It is a fire-year layer, and the rasters are calendar-year layers.}
        \texttt{calendar\_year} is the \emph{majority} year of the object's pixels, while every raster
        above assigns year and month per pixel. A fire straddling 31 December is split across two
        years in the rasters and lands whole in one year here. Neither is wrong, but cross-tabulating
        the two without knowing this produces apparent \enquote{missing} area.
  \item \textbf{Fire year 1998 exists here and in no published raster.} The calendar series starts in
        1999, so the November--December 1998 tail --- 3,845 polygons, about 76 kha --- is only in this
        layer.
  \item \textbf{Area must be summed per object, not per row.} One very large fire year 2000 object
        exceeded Earth Engine's vertex limit on ingest and is stored as four features that each repeat
        the whole object's attributes. Summing \texttt{area\_ha} row by row overstates the layer by
        5.1 Mha.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Using the products in Earth Engine}

The quickest way to see the products is the Earth Engine Code Editor. The snapshot below loads the
annual and monthly layers for one year, the frequency layer for the whole series, and the fire-object
polygons, with the visualisations the platform uses:

\begin{center}
\url{https://code.earthengine.google.com/SNAPSHOT-ID}

\noindent The script it opens is essentially the following, which can also be pasted into a blank
Code Editor tab:

\begin{small}
\begin{verbatim}
var ROOT = 'projects/mapbiomas-argentina/assets/FIRE/COLLECTION-1/FINAL_PRODUCTS/';
var NAME = 'mapbiomas_argentina_fire_collection1_';
var year = 2020;

// Annual burned area: one band per year, 1 = burned.
var annual = ee.Image(ROOT + NAME + 'annual_burned_v2')
               .select('burned_area_' + year).selfMask();
Map.addLayer(annual, {palette: ['#ff0000']}, 'burned ' + year);

// Month of burn: 1-12.
var monthly = ee.Image(ROOT + NAME + 'monthly_burned_v2')
                .select('burned_monthly_' + year).selfMask();
Map.addLayer(monthly, {min: 1, max: 12, palette: ['#2c7bb6','#ffffbf','#d7191c']},
             'month of burn ' + year);

// Fire frequency over the whole series: number of years burned.
var freq = ee.Image(ROOT + NAME + 'frequency_burned_v2')
             .select('fire_frequency_1999_2025');
Map.addLayer(freq, {min: 1, max: 10}, 'fire frequency');

// The fire-object vector database: one feature per mapped fire.
var fires = ee.FeatureCollection(ROOT + 'burned_area_polygons_v2')
              .filterDate(year + '-01-01', (year + 1) + '-01-01');
var style = {color: 'white', fillColor: '00000000', width: 1};
Map.addLayer(fires.style(style), {}, 'fire objects ' + year);

Map.setCenter(-64, -34, 5);
\end{verbatim}
\end{small}

\textbf{band} of it, and the polygon layer is filtered by date rather than by a year property,
because each fire carries a single timestamp taken from its median burn date.

\section{Limitations and known challenges}

\subsection{Where the algorithm struggles}

\textbf{Productive, actively managed landscapes are the hardest case.} In agricultural regions the
region-growing step produces poor clusters: harvest patches enter the candidate footprint and are
occasionally merged into a genuine scar. No spatial strategy can repair this, because the ambiguity
is already present in the per-observation probability --- a harvested field and a burned field look
alike to a single-date spectral model. Improving fire mapping in croplands and sown pastures
requires better \emph{spectral} models, which in turn requires collecting training data specifically
on those confusions.

The object classifier was conceived from the pilot's experience, where many false positives were
easily separable from fire by shape and seed density: dendritic, sparse objects associated with
drought and volcanic ash. It does that job well, and it also removes clearing-type disturbances ---
roads, urbanisation --- that the pilot confounded with fire. But in regions where the false
positives are compact and densely seeded, it helps much less. It is a filter on \emph{patch
geometry and density}, and it cannot recover information the spectral stage did not capture.

\textbf{Understorey fires in the most productive systems remain largely undetected}, as they were in
the pilot: a fire that burns beneath an intact canopy leaves little signal in a 30 m
top-of-canopy reflectance series.

\subsection{Limitations inherited from the data}

\textbf{The object classifier's labelled sample is the binding limitation of the whole spatial
stage} (Section~\ref{sec-objlabels}). Until a randomly sampled set of labels exists, the deployed
thresholds must be treated as a lower bound and the per-object probability as a ranking rather than
as a calibrated probability.

\textbf{Observation density varies enormously through the series}, and the algorithm handles it
explicitly --- an adaptive window choice, an adaptive gap gate, and an exported quality band --- but
handling is not equivalence. Detections in 1999--2001, when only two sensors were flying and one of
them had just launched, rest on fewer observations than detections in 2015. A residual increasing
trend in the fire-object rate survives the removal of the era-proxy predictor; it is
\textbf{unattributed rather than demonstrated to be clean}, and it should not be published as a
fire-regime finding without an independent record to check it against.

\textbf{The edge fire years are partial by construction} (Section~\ref{sec-fireyear}): 1998 holds
only four months and 2025 only eight. Completing them requires extending the temporal stage back to
May 1998 and forward through 2026.

\textbf{The land cover used to select models is frozen}. The \texttt{veg\_fire} classification comes
from a specific MapBiomas LULC collection, deliberately held fixed, because it decides which model
judges each pixel and therefore the entire downstream candidate set. A newer land-cover collection
reassigns that wherever a class changed, so the layer cannot be half-migrated: either the whole
collection moves or none of it does.

\subsection{Limitations of shape}

\textbf{A fire that crosses 31 December becomes two scars} in the published calendar-year products,
because year and month are assigned per pixel. Within the algorithm the fire is one object in one
fire year; the split is a property of the delivery format, and the fire-object layer preserves the
undivided event.

\textbf{The minimum mapped unit is one hectare.} Smaller objects are scored like everything else but
are not published; they are 3.4\,\% of the objects and 0.044\,\% of the area.

\section{Priority lines of improvement}

These are listed in what we judge to be decreasing order of effect on mapping accuracy. They do not
consider incorporating sensors other than Landsat, since for the first collections the priority is
to refine the mapping without compromising the temporal extent of the product.

\paragraph{Training data that represents the false positives.} As in the pilot, the largest
available gain is data on the processes that mimic fire --- harvest, drought, flooding, logging,
insect defoliation, ash deposition --- collected explicitly as negatives, region by region. The
Collection 1 training set already contains some of these, and they demonstrably help; there are
nowhere near enough of them. Because these processes are spectrally similar to burning, the
\emph{relative} abundance of each class in the training set matters as much as the absolute number
of samples: the less separable the classes, the more the prior implied by the sampling drives the
fitted probability.

\paragraph{A randomly sampled set of object labels.} The second-largest gain costs the least effort.
A few thousand labels drawn at random from the object population --- rather than from where fires
were known --- would convert the object classifier's output from a ranking into a calibrated
probability, put the size-band thresholds on a defensible footing, and let the population fire rate
be estimated rather than assumed. Objects with wide posterior intervals are the natural targets for
a second round.

\paragraph{Better use of the temporal metrics in the segmentation.} The sixteen exported bands were
fixed before the segmentation stage existed, and some of them are never read. The interesting
question is not only which to drop but \textbf{which the segmentation should have been using}: the
seed and candidate cuts currently rest on the jump metrics and the observation count, leaving the
sharpness and the window-span bands --- the ones that best separate abrupt change from gradual
change --- almost unused. This is the cheapest available improvement in commission error, because it
requires no re-export.

\paragraph{Splitting the vegetation remap.} Several \texttt{veg\_fire} classes are currently merged
across regions for want of data rather than on ecological grounds (Section~\ref{sec-vegfire}). With
more collected fires, those groups can be split and fitted on their own merits, which directly
improves the per-class fit where it is weakest.

\paragraph{The spectral model itself.} The logistic regression can be extended to a categorical
regression predicting the probability of several classes at once --- unburned, burned, cloud, water,
ash, clearing, drought --- instead of a binary one; or simple auxiliary models can be multiplied
into it, for instance a brightness-based probability of cloud, snow or ash. Both remain compatible
with the constraint that the model has to run inside GEE over the entire archive. This is also where
richer classifiers would pay off most, if a deployment path for them existed.

\paragraph{Provenance stamping.} A practical, cheap improvement: every exported annual-metrics asset
records its year and its tile but \emph{not} which coefficient set produced it. Because the
coefficients enter through a per-pixel remap, a model improvement affecting one vegetation class
changes nothing in tiles where that class is absent, so a partial re-export is technically sound ---
but it would be of undocumented provenance. Stamping the deployed model version on every asset costs
nothing and is what makes a partial re-run auditable.

\section{Considerations for Collection 2}

Re-running the temporal stage is the single largest commitment a collection can make --- on the
order of a terabyte and weeks of distributed export --- so the decisions that force it belong on the
table early, with time to think, rather than in the run-up to a launch. Three of them are already
identified: whether to recompute against a newer land-cover collection, which reassigns
\texttt{veg\_fire} everywhere a class changed and therefore cannot be done by halves; whether a
model improvement may be applied to part of the country, which is technically possible and currently
blocked only by the provenance-stamping gap above; and which of the sixteen bands to keep.

Two metrics should simply stop being computed. The mean observation count per object is not a
predictor and carrying it invites the era-proxy mistake; the observation count belongs where it is
already used and normalised, inside the seed definition. And pixel count should give way entirely to
area, since the pixel scale is latitude-dependent and the model confirms that the count carries
nothing the area does not.

Beyond that, the two open fronts are the ones named above: training data on the confounding
processes, and a randomly sampled set of object labels. Both are collection effort rather than
algorithmic development, and both are the kind of work whose payoff is invisible until it is done.
